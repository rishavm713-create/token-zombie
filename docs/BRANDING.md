## 7. Legal background (summary as of November 2026, not legal advice)

**Confirmed law.** This is general, well-established law, not a ruling on this project:

**Copyright, India.** The Copyright Act, 1957 treats computer programs as literary works (s. 2(o)), and the author is the first owner (s. 17). Copyright arises automatically on creation, with no registration needed. Section 57 gives authors a right to claim authorship (moral right). Through the Berne Convention, which India and most countries have joined, copyright is also recognized abroad without registration.

**Licensing.** By licensing under MIT, the copyright owner has already allowed copying, modification and resale. Copyright cannot be used to stop resale of MIT code that keeps its notices.

**Trademarks, India.**
- The Trade Marks Act, 1999 and the Trade Marks Rules, 2017 govern trademarks. 2026 changes reported so far are procedural (Nice classification, e-filing, Registry practice) plus penalty changes under the Jan Vishwas amendments. Check the official texts on ipindia.gov.in before relying on them.
- Registration gives the exclusive right to use the mark for the registered goods and services, and the right to sue for infringement (ss. 28-29).
- An unregistered mark cannot be sued on for infringement. Its owner can still bring a **passing-off** action (s. 27(2)), which requires proving goodwill, a misrepresentation and damage.
- Describing a product honestly by its brand name is generally allowed (s. 30).
- Falsely representing an unregistered mark as registered (for example, using ®) is not allowed.

**International.** Trademark rights are **territorial**. There is no automatic worldwide protection.
- The **Paris Convention** gives national treatment, a 6-month priority period for filing a trademark in other member countries (Art. 4), protection for well-known marks (Art. 6bis) and a duty to stop unfair competition (Art. 10bis).
- **TRIPS** sets minimum trademark standards for WTO members (Arts. 15-21).
- The **Madrid Protocol**, which India joined in 2013, lets an owner file one international application through the Indian office, based on an Indian application or registration, and designate chosen member countries. Each designated country still examines it and may refuse it. For 5 years it depends on the Indian base mark. It is not one worldwide registration.

**Possible arguments, not settled outcomes:**
- A seller who sells a fork as "TOKEN ZOMBIE" or claims to have created it may be liable for passing off, false endorsement or unfair competition.
- Removing the original notice may breach the MIT condition, so the copyright licence would not cover that copy.
- Whether such claims would succeed depends on the facts and the country: evidence of goodwill, of confusion and of where the product is sold.

**Not possible under MIT:**
- forcing every fork to rename
- forcing a creator credit in a fork's marketing
- charging royalties
- forbidding commercial resale

These can only come from trademark law, where it applies, or from a signed agreement.
